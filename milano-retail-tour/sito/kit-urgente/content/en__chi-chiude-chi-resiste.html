Who closes and who holds on: what bookshops and pharmacies teach specialist stores

2 November 2026 · MiRT Radar · Specialist retail

Between 2012 and 2025, across the 122 Italian cities tracked by Confcommercio, 156,000 retail outlets disappeared, counting shops and market stalls, and in 2025 the pace rose to 3.1% a year. Two models are bucking the trend: the pharmacy, which has become a hub of services, and the American independent bookshop, reborn as a community space. The lesson for opticians, perfumeries, jewellers and hardware stores is to sell a service and a place, not just a product.

The numbers behind empty high streets

The Confcommercio study published in March 2026 measures the loss of shops in Italian town centres: in 2025 it reached 3.1% a year, up from 2.2% in previous surveys. In historic centres, newsagents fell by 51.9%, clothing and footwear by 36.9%, furniture and hardware by 35.9%, books and toys by 32.6%.

Over the same period restaurants grew by 35% and short-term rentals by 184%. Town centres are not emptying: they are changing function. Pharmacies and computer and phone shops are also growing, although the study gives no percentages.

Milan is no exception. Retail and food-service businesses in the city numbered 46,343 in February 2026, 1,586 fewer than in April 2025. According to Nomisma, 755 neighbourhood shops closed in the metropolitan area over ten years, while commercial rents rose by 16%.

The pharmacy: from shop to health hub

Italy has 20,295 pharmacies. According to Federfarma’s report “La farmacia italiana 2026”, 78.9% offer tests and rapid diagnostics and 77.4% offer electrocardiograms. In 2025, 8,729 pharmacies delivered more than 826,000 telemedicine services.

The result shows in loyalty: 81.7% of Italians always go to the same pharmacy. A service creates a reason to come back that price alone does not.

The bookshop: from shelf to town square

In the United States, where Amazon started by selling books, the American Booksellers Association now has 3,417 members with 3,783 locations: more than 500 more than a year ago, and nearly triple the figure of a decade ago.

Kelley Hartnett, owner of Double Dog Bookshop in Wentzville, Missouri, put it this way to Fortune: “For me, Double Dog is about maybe 50% books and 50% community.” She added: “People are craving connection, especially in-person connection.”

What it means for your store

The internet sells products too. It cannot sell a service or a place. A few ideas to adapt:

  • Opticians: eye tests and frame adjustments by appointment, with a reminder for the next check-up.
  • Perfumeries: bookable fragrance consultations or skin analysis, small evening workshops.
  • Jewellers: free cleaning and checks, repairs, valuation and trade-in of pre-owned pieces.
  • Hardware stores: key cutting, made-to-measure cuts, Saturday morning classes, tool hire.
  • Bookshops and stationers: reading groups, author talks, children’s workshops.

Many stores already do these things, for free and without saying so. The next step is to make them visible, bookable and repeatable.

Where AI comes in

A service creates appointments, due dates and customer histories. That is exactly the data AI helps with: reminding customers of their next check-up, inviting people who bought a brand to the right evening, drafting the message a member of staff then personalises. But the service comes first: without it, AI has nothing to remember.

What to do on Monday

  1. List the services you already offer for free and do not talk about.
  2. Pick one and make it bookable: on your Google profile, on your website or on WhatsApp.
  3. For eight weeks, measure how many customers come back because of it.
